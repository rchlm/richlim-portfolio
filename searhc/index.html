One SEARHC Experience

Southeast Alaska Regional Health Consortium serves patients scattered across a network of island and mainland communities, many reachable only by plane or ferry. The ask was to take a fragmented patient portal and rebuild it into a single mobile-first experience that could hold the whole system together.

Overview

SEARHC's existing patient portal ran on top of Meditech, its underlying EHR, with scheduling and notifications handled separately by Luma. Patients experienced the seams. The brief was to design a unified app that felt like one product, not a stitched-together set of systems, for a population that includes remote communities, lower digital literacy users, and Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian patients whose relationship to the health system carries its own cultural weight.

My Role

UX / Design Lead

Leading design on the engagement in partnership with Raining Virtue, the delivery partner handling infrastructure and technical architecture. Owned discovery research, information architecture, and the identity and access model, working directly with SEARHC's compliance, clinical, and IT stakeholders across a dozen-plus discovery sessions.

The Problem

The portal's problems weren't visual. They were structural. Patient identity and digital account identity had never been treated as separate concepts, which made proxy access, minor accounts, and multi-facility records behave inconsistently. Behavioral health data carried federal confidentiality restrictions the rest of the app didn't. And every design assumption had to be checked against what Meditech actually did, not what the team assumed it did.

How do you design a single digital front door for a health system that spans dozens of communities, an aging EHR, and layers of legal complexity around who's allowed to see what?

What We Did

01

Untangling Patient Identity from Digital Identity

The existing system conflated a person's clinical record with their app login. We worked with SEARHC's identity architect to separate the two: an enterprise patient identity that exists independent of any account, and a digital identity that links to it. That split unlocked three distinct patient journeys, including one that lets a patient schedule and receive care without ever creating an account.

02

An Age-Banded Proxy & Access Model

Caregiver and guardian access needed to satisfy HIPAA, Alaska minor consent law, and Meditech's own configuration limits at once. We built a proxy framework around two hard thresholds, ages 13 and 18, where access is granted, staff-mediated, or severed. What looked like a permissions toggle turned out to require its own lifecycle logic.

03

A Dashboard Rebuilt Around Real Usage

The original design leaned on a single notification-pill pattern to surface everything needing attention. We replaced it with a true dashboard: structured cards for appointments, medications, results, and messages, each with its own empty state. Card priority was set by actual usage data, not assumption, which is why medications outranked billing.

04

Designing With Cultural Weight, Not Around It

A meaningful share of SEARHC's patients are Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian. We set a hard rule early: no formline art or language elements without community ownership. Any indigenous design element gets commissioned through local artists and reviewed with the Sealaska Heritage Institute, not generated by the design team in isolation.

05

Discovery Across a Fragmented System Stack

Meditech, Luma, Zoom's telehealth infrastructure, and a Salesforce-based travel workflow all had to be reconciled into one coherent architecture. Repeated discovery sessions surfaced gaps between what stakeholders assumed a system did and what it actually did, most notably around adolescent self-registration, which reset a core piece of the access model.

Ongoing

Engagement in active delivery, building on the discovery foundation

The research phase reframed the project before a single screen was finalized. What started as a portal refresh became an identity and access architecture problem first, a navigation problem second, and a visual design problem third. That ordering shaped everything that followed.

What I Learned

Healthcare research rewards patience over instinct. The assumption that felt obviously right in the room — that teens should self-manage their own access — turned out to be wrong the moment we checked it against how the EHR actually behaved. This project taught me to treat every design decision in a regulated system as a hypothesis to verify, not a call to make on judgment alone.
